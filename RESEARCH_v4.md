# Hourly levels beat every intraday setup tested

The owner's own idea is the best lead the bot has. Front-running support on **hourly** swing levels produced about **6.9 trades a week at +0.27% per trade after costs (t = 2.09)** across CCJ, MU and PLTR over two years of data. It was the only short-term setup in this session's backtests that came out positive. Every intraday momentum and breakout rule lost money, and the overnight "edge" disappeared once tested over a decade with costs. The setup roughly quadruples trade count over the daily-level version, but a stronger edge per trade it is not. At the largest size a $1,000 cash account can carry (about half the account per trade, risking under 1%), a simulation of it averages roughly **+1% a week if the backtest edge holds in full, and +0.5% if it shrinks by half**, as published edges typically do. It lands one week in seven at +5% or better and one in fourteen at −5% or worse. Hitting +5% *every* week at this edge would take about **$18,500 of position turnover a week**, while cash-account settlement caps turnover near $5,000 and margin requires $2,000 of equity. So the way to build toward the goal is an opportunity engine. Run the hourly-level setup live with the daily-level one alongside. Shadow-test the next setups before they risk money. Hold the core, because that is where the overnight return lives. Use Claude for news vetoes and loss post-mortems rather than for forecasting or sizing. Move the bot from GitHub Actions cron to an always-on VM with websockets. Replace day and week loss brakes with fixed-fraction sizing and operational kill switches, so the bot keeps trading after a bad day without risking ruin.

## Five percent a week needs about 18 times weekly turnover the account cannot reach

The target is concrete once compounded. 1.05^52 is about **12.6x a year**. Renaissance's Medallion Fund, the best record on file, averaged **39.1% a year net** from 1988 to 2018 ([WealthManagement.com](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/alternative-investments/jim-simons-built-the-best-hedge-fund-ever)). The retail base rates point the same way. The most active of 66,465 discount-broker households earned **11.4% a year against the market's 17.9%** ([Barber & Odean, Journal of Finance 2000](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:55:y:2000:i:2:p:773-806)). Among Brazilians who day traded for 300+ days, **97% lost money**, with "no evidence that individuals learn from day trading" ([Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fgv/eesptd/525.html)). The best-documented single-stock intraday strategy in the literature returned **41.6% a year**, using up to 4x leverage across the 20 most active stocks each day ([Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz 2024](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/3c2989c4-688d-4d78-8a71-f02690990d51/content)). That is under 1% a week before decay and before removing the leverage.

The session's own backtests turn that into a turnover problem. At +0.27% per trade, a $50 weekly gain on $1,000 requires about **$18,500 of positions opened and closed every week**, or 18.5 times the account ([own backtest on Yahoo Finance hourly data](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/PLTR/history/)). A cash account cannot reach that. Stock trades settle T+1, and a **good-faith violation** occurs when "a position is opened using unsettled funds and then the position is subsequently closed." Three GFVs in 12 months bring a 90-day settled-cash restriction ([E*TRADE](https://us.etrade.com/knowledge/library/stocks/understanding-cash-account-violations)). In practice that limits the bot to roughly one full-account round trip per day, about **$5,000 a week**. At +0.27% per trade, that is about 1.35% a week at the absolute ceiling. Margin would lift the ceiling, but margin loans require **$2,000 minimum equity** ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/06/26/wall-street-scrapped-a-25-year-old-rule-does-it-ma/)). The Robinhood Gold margin feature should therefore stay disabled in the bot's config until the account clears that bar. The Pattern Day Trader rule is no longer the constraint. Alpaca lifted it on **June 4, 2026**, removing day-trade counting and the $25,000 minimum ([Alpaca blog](https://alpaca.markets/blog/finra-retires-the-pdt-rule-introducing-alpacas-new-intraday-margin-framework/)).

To show what is reachable, I simulated the hourly-level setup at different sizes. The model reproduces the backtest's 31% win rate and +0.27% average, uses a −1.7% loss (0.4% front-run plus the 1.2% stop plus 0.1% cost), and runs 7 trades a week for a year, 10,000 times. A half-account position with that stop risks about **0.85% of equity per trade**, inside the conventional 1% rule. That works because the hourly stop is tight, not because the position is small.

| Size per trade | Edge assumed | Avg week | Weeks ≥ +5% | Weeks ≤ −5% | Median year | Chance of losing year | Median max drawdown |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20% (backtest's ~$200) | full +0.27% | +0.38% | 0.5% | ~0% | 1.21x | 4% | 7% |
| 50% (cash-account max) | full +0.27% | +0.95% | 14% | 7% | 1.58x | 5% | 17% |
| 50% | halved +0.135% | +0.47% | 14% | 7% | 1.24x | 21% | 19% |
| 100% (needs margin/overlap) | full +0.27% | +1.90% | 38% | 31% | 2.31x | 7% | 31% |

The simulation is optimistic in two ways. Trades are drawn independently, but the three stocks are correlated. It also ignores stops gapped through overnight, and the worst single nights in the decade of data ran **−13% to −18%** ([own backtest, Yahoo daily data](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)). A half-size position caught in one such gap loses 6–9% of the account at once. The practical reading for the owner is that half-size positions on the hourly setup are the most aggressive defensible way to pursue his goal. They produce real +5% weeks about once every seven weeks while keeping the median drawdown under 20%. The 100% row shows that doubling size doubles the average week but more than doubles the pain. In that case, **more than half of simulated years see a 30% drawdown**. The route to sustained +5% weeks runs through growing the account and proving the edge, not through more size today.

## The owner's hourly level idea is the only short-term setup that survived testing

The session tested eight short-term setups on two years of hourly bars, pooled across CCJ, MU and PLTR with a 0.10% round-trip cost. The results were lopsided ([own backtest, Yahoo Finance hourly data](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)). Holding from the open to the close lost 0.03% a day over 2,165 trades. Buying after a first hour up more than 1% lost 0.07% (t = −3.15). First-hour range breakouts lost 0.08%. A 5-minute opening range breakout lost 0.33% per trade with a 28% win rate over the 60 days of data available. Gap-down buys earned +0.25% (t = 1.31) but flipped to −0.25% when restricted to uptrends, an instability that signals noise. Holding overnight, from close to the next open, looked spectacular at **+0.28% a night, t = 5.24**. On daily data from 2015 to 2026, though, the same trade turned MU's +1,887% buy-and-hold into +119% and PLTR's +1,549% into −19% after a 0.10% round-trip cost. At 0.2% cost every stock lost 83–97%. The overnight return is real; the academic version is that momentum earns **+0.98% a month overnight and about zero intraday** ([Lou, Polk & Skouras, JFE 2019](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/TugOfWar.pdf)). But a position held continuously earns it for free. Round-tripping it every night hands it to the spread. **The core sleeve is the overnight strategy.**

That pattern also explains why the hourly-level setup works where intraday rules fail. Limiting its holds to one day cut the edge from +0.27% to **+0.05%**, while a five-day hold kept +0.24%. The setup earns its money by holding overnight across one to three sessions. The bot should therefore be a **short-swing trader that enters on hourly levels and holds overnight**, not a day trader that goes flat at 4 PM.

| Hourly-level variant (front-run 0.4% above zone, stop 1.2% under, target next hourly resistance) | Trades/week | Win | Avg/trade | t |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reward ≥ 1.5x risk, hold ≤ 3 days | 6.9 | 31% | +0.27% | 2.09 |
| Reward ≥ 2x, hold ≤ 3 days | 5.7 | 28% | +0.20% | 1.40 |
| Reward ≥ 3x | 3.4 | 23% | +0.07% | 0.36 |
| No front-run (limit exactly at zone) | 7.0 | 25% | +0.18% | 1.62 |
| Stop 2% under zone | 3.1 | 33% | +0.32% | 1.31 |
| Uptrend only | 3.1 | — | +0.12% | — |
| Hold ≤ 1 day / ≤ 5 days | 6.4 / 5.6 | — | +0.05% / +0.24% | — |

Three findings in that table deserve weight. First, front-running the zone by 0.4% lifted the win rate from 25% to 28% compared with resting the order exactly at the level. That matches the order-clustering mechanism Carol Osler documented in currency markets, where take-profit orders bunch *at* round numbers (**9.3% executed exactly at "00" versus 4.4% of stop-losses**) and stops sit *just beyond* them, triggering "in waves" once a level breaks ([FRBNY Staff Report 125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.html); [Staff Report 150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.html)). Applying Osler's FX evidence to equities is still an extrapolation. Second, adding an uptrend filter *halved* the edge, a warning against piling on filters that sound sensible. Third, CCJ contributed little in every variant: +0.03% in the 2x version, +0.13% in the 1.5x version and −0.42% with the wider stop. MU and PLTR carried the result. A fresh **US$1 billion at-the-market equity program** filed November 5 gives Cameco a supply of stock that agents can sell into rallies ([SEC SUPPL](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1009001/000119312526450176/d817484dsuppl.htm)). That offers a plausible reason CCJ bounces are weaker. Per-stock samples are small, though, so CCJ should get a lower weight through the shrinkage rules below, not be dropped.

The honest caveat is multiple testing. Ten variants were tried, and the best t of 2.09 sits below the **t > 3.0** bar Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue new findings should clear after widespread data mining ([NBER w20592](https://nber.org/papers/w20592)). Out of sample, published predictors return **26% less, and 58% less after publication** ([McLean & Pontiff via SUFE](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/359722)). The right move is to pre-commit to one variant (reward ≥ 1.5x, hold ≤ 3 days) and stop tuning it. The live expectation should be about half the backtest. The setup also has a known structural flaw. Resting limit buys are **adversely selected**: they fill when price keeps falling and miss when it bounces early, which made Finnish retail investors' trades look mechanically worse ([Linnainmaa, Journal of Finance 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v65y2010i4p1473-1506.html)). Two practices limit the damage. Cancel the order if an hourly bar closes below the zone before it fills. Log every "touched but unfilled" and "filled then stopped" event. Paper results will flatter the setup because Alpaca's paper engine **does not simulate queue position** for resting limits ([Alpaca Paper Trading](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading)). Count a fill only when price trades through the limit by at least a tick.

The daily-level version should stay as a complement, not be replaced. It produced about 1.5 trades a week at a ~15% win rate, with average wins of +14% against losses of −1.5%, for **+0.89% per trade** ([own backtest](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/CCJ/history/)). It is a lottery-shaped strategy. The chance of 20 straight losses at a 15% win rate is about 4%, so it needs small size and patience. Together, the two give the bot roughly **8–9 live trades a week**, against about 1.5 before.

The ORB result carries a lesson for the whole engine. Nearly all of its edge comes from *choosing which stock to trade*, the top 20 by relative volume out of about 7,000. A three-stock universe can only borrow that edge on its own rare high-volume days. RVOL computed from Alpaca's free feed uses IEX prints, which are about **2.5% of US volume** ([Alpaca Docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/historical-stock-data-1)). Compute it consistently on that one feed, or from SIP data older than 15 minutes.

The owner should also know how slowly any setup can be confirmed. With the hourly setup's per-trade spread, an independent confirmation at t = 2 needs about **650 trades, roughly two years at 7 a week**. That figure is my arithmetic from the backtest's t-statistic. Six months of live trading, about 180 trades, can only catch a badly broken edge, not prove a good one. Frequency still helps a great deal, because metrics like fill-through rate, slippage against the midpoint and adverse-selection rate converge in weeks rather than years.

## Options, whale and news data make good vetoes and poor triggers

No public feed shows other traders' resting stops, iceberg size or dark-pool intent, so "whale" signals from free data are inferences. Where the academic edges exist, they sit mostly in small or hard-to-borrow stocks. Excluding high-borrow-fee stocks cuts IV-spread and skew predictability by **at least two-thirds** ([Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/n73cyx89gs)). For liquid names, option-driven price pressure shows "nothing significant after day 1" ([DOTS paper](https://www.abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2016/annual-conference/investment-finance/127-informed-trading-in-options-or-price-pressure-in-stocks-connecting-the-dots-in-option-based-return-predictability.pdf)). Four uses survive. The first is an **IV skew veto**: stocks with the steepest put smirk lag by **10.9% a year** ([Xing, Zhang & Zhao](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v45y2010i03p641-662_00.html)), so the bot should skip long entries when skew sits in the top decile of its own one-year history. The second is a **dealer-gamma volatility gauge**. Positive dealer gamma dampens volatility ([NPPWK, Review of Financial Studies 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v34y2021i4p1952-1986..html)), so negative or near-zero estimated GEX should mean wider stops and smaller size. It should never set direction, because in call-heavy PLTR the usual sign assumption can flip. The third is **insider open-market buys** (Form 4 code P), which earn about **82 bp a month** when opportunistic ([NBER Digest on Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski](https://www.nber.org/digest/apr11/decoding-inside-information)). PLTR's filings are dominated by 10b5-1 planned sales, so expect only a handful of real signals a year. The fourth is a **retail-herding "don't chase" filter**, because extreme retail herding is followed by about **−6% over five days** ([Barber et al.](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/SSRN-id3715077.pdf)). Congressional trades "underperform or, at best, match" the market ([Chen & Sacerdote 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35041)), and 13F filings arrive 45 days late, so both should be ignored. Free yfinance chains carry no greeks and no history ([Macroption](https://www.macroption.com/yahoo-finance-options-python/)). The step that unlocks all of this is to start snapshotting chains twice a day now, one snapshot after 09:45 ET when the prior day's open interest is published ([ThetaData](https://thetadata.net/docs/operations/option_history_open_interest.html)), and to z-score each feature against the stock's own history.

News works the same way. Large-cap prices absorb public news within about a minute, and only traders acting within about **15 seconds** profited from CNBC mentions ([Busse & Green via Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/real-time-market-efficiency/)). A retail Claude pipeline will not win that race. What it can do is judge whether news is genuinely new. Bad news drifts, while extreme moves on *no* news reverse ([Chan, JFE 2003](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:70:y:2003:i:2:p:223-260)). Returns on stale, recycled news reverse the following week, especially where retail trades heavily ([Tetlock, RFS 2011](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v24y2011i5p1481-1512.html)). That gives the news agent three jobs. It blocks hourly-level buys after fresh negative news, which is also the condition under which the Novy-Marx reversal evidence says pullbacks stop working. It flags sharp moves on stale news as fade candidates. It enforces an earnings stand-aside. MU and PLTR file earnings 8-Ks at about **4:02 and 4:06 PM ET**. Cameco files **6-K rather than 8-K**, with its Q3 release reaching EDGAR around 6:57 AM ET ([data.sec.gov PLTR](https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK0001321655.json); [CCJ](https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK0001009001.json)). EDGAR's submissions JSON is free and allows 10 requests a second with a declared User-Agent ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/os/accessing-edgar-data)).

The LLM-trading evidence supports exactly that narrow role. GPT-4 headline scores predicted 1–2 day drift, but the **Sharpe ratio decayed from 6.54 in late 2021 to 1.22 by early 2024** and the strategy was **unprofitable at 20 bp round-trip costs** ([Lopez-Lira & Tang](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). After the model's training cutoff, FinMem's return fell about **72%**, and a costed reproduction of TradingAgents dropped its Sharpe from 0.43 to **0.22, below buy-and-hold** ([The Alpha Illusion review](https://www.themoonlight.io/fr/review/the-alpha-illusion-reported-alpha-from-llm-trading-agents-should-not-be-treated-as-deployment-evidence)). In a 2004–2024 test, LLM agents were "too conservative in rising markets and too aggressive in falling ones" ([FINSABER, arXiv 2505.07078](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.07078v1)). An August 2026 study ran 102 GPT-4.1 strategy-search trials. It returned **+4.7% out of sample against +40.8% for buy-and-hold**, and claude-sonnet-5 reached the same verdict ([Gençay, arXiv 2608.27734](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27734)). In live competition, Claude Sonnet 4.5 lost **42%** in Alpha Arena's first season ([Forklog](https://forklog.com/en/four-out-of-six-ai-models-suffer-losses-in-trading-tournament/)). The conclusion is consistent: LLM confidence is poorly calibrated, so it must never size positions. Claude works best as an auditable extractor that hands structured flags to deterministic code.

For CCJ, besides the ATM program, Sprott's uranium trust trades at a **17.2% discount to NAV** and is not buying spot ([Sprott](https://www.sprott.com/investment-strategies/physical-commodity-funds/uranium/)). For MU, TrendForce forecasts 4Q26 **DRAM contract prices up 10–15%** ([TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20260930-13258.html)). PLTR *fell* after beating and raising on November 2, on valuation concerns ([TradingView/GuruFocus](https://www.tradingview.com/news/gurufocus:b21fd39ab094b:0-palantir-technologies-posts-record-q3-revenue-but-shares-fall-on-valuation-concerns)). ## Every loss gets classified, but only process errors change rules

Learning from every loss is the right instinct. The trap is that a few trades a week cannot tell a good setup from a bad one for a long time. After 20 trades, an observed 60% win rate is consistent with a true rate anywhere from about 38% to 82%. Separating a 55% setup from a 45% one takes about **190 trades per setup** (binomial arithmetic from the learning-loop research). LLMs also readily write convincing stories for random losses. The evidence on adaptive reweighting is sobering. Cederburg and coauthors tested 103 strategies whose volatility-managed versions looked good in-sample. Run in real time, they "do not systematically beat their unmanaged counterparts," because the estimated scaling parameters were unstable ([Alpha Architect on Cederburg et al., JFE 2020](https://alphaarchitect.com/does-portfolio-timing-based-on-volatility-signals-outperform-buy-and-hold/)). Strategy momentum is real for academic factors, which earn about **1 bp a month after a losing year versus 53 bp after a winning year** ([Ehsani & Linnainmaa, NBER w25551](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25551)). But that persistence plays out over months to a year, not over the last five trades.

The loop that fits this evidence has four parts. The first is a **post-mortem on every closed trade, winners included**. Claude fills a fixed JSON schema: whether the entry met every rule, slippage against plan, a regime tag, any news shock with a timestamp and whether it was knowable, and exactly one loss class. The classes are (1) rule violation, (2) bad execution, (3) regime mismatch, (4) unforeseeable news shock and (5) noise, a valid trade that lost within expected odds. Only classes 1 and 2 justify a process fix such as a bug fix, a tighter checklist or a better order type. Class 5 changes nothing. Class 3 feeds statistics that act only after many tagged trades. Class 4 can prompt a news-blackout rule that the owner decides. Annie Duke calls the mistake this prevents "resulting," equating decision quality with outcome ([Maven on Duke](https://maven.com/articles/decision-making-mistake)). The loop should also audit the share of losses labelled noise. With a 31% win rate, most losses *should* be noise, and an agent that rarely says so is over-explaining.

The second part is **Bayesian shrinkage**. Each setup's win rate is modelled as Beta(10 + wins, 10 + losses), or centred on the backtest rate, so that a 2-for-6 start reads as about 43% rather than 33%. Allocation code consumes these posteriors and the loss-class counts, never Claude's free text. The third part is **bounded tilts**. Setup weights move at most ±25–50% from base and no more than weekly. There are no automatic changes below 30 trades per setup, and no setup goes to zero on performance alone. That last rule matters for CCJ and for the daily-level setup, whose losing streaks are long by design. The fourth part is a **trial ledger**. Every rule or parameter change, including Claude-suggested ones, is logged as a trial, because each tweak after a loss spends statistical budget. Deflated Sharpe corrects for trial counts ([Bailey & López de Prado, SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)). It cannot catch look-ahead leakage, though: a planted future-peeking oracle scored a perfect DSR of 1.00 ([Gençay](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27734)). Features must therefore be built so they *cannot* see future bars.

Regime detection belongs in the loop only as a slow sizing input. A persistence-penalized jump model cut S&P 500 drawdown from −55% to −27% with a higher Sharpe. A plain hidden Markov model produced "numerous short-lived regimes that are unintuitive and difficult to trade" ([Shu, Yu & Mulvey, arXiv 2402.05272](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2402.05272)). The session's own uptrend filter cut the hourly setup's edge, so regime tags should be recorded on every trade but should gate nothing until hundreds of tagged trades show a difference. One hard boundary keeps the loop safe. Per-trade risk, stop logic (which may only tighten automatically), position caps, the ticker list, the long-only rule, margin use and the earnings rule live in versioned config that only the owner edits. Automatic changes may reduce risk. Increases need the owner.

## Always-on hosting reacts in seconds for $0–6 a month

GitHub Actions cron is the wrong engine for continuous trading. GitHub's docs say scheduled runs "can be delayed during periods of high loads," especially at the top of the hour, and "some queued jobs may be dropped." The shortest interval is **5 minutes** ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/workflows-and-actions/events-that-trigger-workflows)). That matches the owner's experience of late runs arriving in bursts. Jobs cap at **6 hours** ([GitHub limits](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/limits)), so a full session needs two chained jobs. A full session every trading day on a private repo would cost about $17–50 a month against the free plan's **$12 of included compute** ([GitHub billing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/concepts/product-billing/github-actions)). The better host is an always-on VM. Google Cloud's free tier includes one **e2-micro** in us-east1, near the exchanges ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/free/docs/free-cloud-features)). Oracle's Always Free A1 was cut to **2 OCPU / 12 GB** in June 2026, and Oracle can reclaim instances whose CPU, network and memory all stay under 20% for a week, which a tiny bot would trigger ([Oracle Docs](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/FreeTier/resourceref.htm); [InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/07/oracle-cloud-free-tier-limits/)). DigitalOcean's **$6/month** droplet with 1 GB is the predictable paid choice ([DigitalOcean](https://www.digitalocean.com/pricing/droplets)). Render's free tier sleeps after 15 minutes and does not support background workers ([Render](https://render.com/docs/free)).

On the VM, the bot runs under systemd with `Restart=always` and keeps two websockets open in one asyncio loop. The first is alpaca-py's `StockDataStream` on the free IEX feed, which delivers real-time trades, quotes and minute bars for up to 30 symbols. The second is `TradingStream`, which pushes fills, cancels and rejections within seconds ([Alpaca streaming docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/real-time-stock-pricing-data); [WebSocket streaming](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/websocket-streaming)). alpaca-py 0.45.0 reconnects with exponential backoff, and its optional `data_timeout` catches sockets that stay open but go silent ([alpaca-py docs](https://alpaca.markets/sdks/python/market_data.html)). Set it to 2–5 minutes, since IEX can go quiet briefly. The free plan allows **one connection per endpoint**, so a leftover GitHub Action running the same code would knock the VM off with error 406 ([Alpaca Docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/streaming-market-data)). The bot backfills hourly and 5-minute bars at startup with `feed=iex`, or SIP ending at least 15 minutes ago ([Alpaca FAQ](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/market-data-faq)), then builds bars from the stream. It sends ntfy alerts on fills, reconnects and exceptions, plus a heartbeat every 30 minutes, which stays well inside ntfy's **250 messages a day** ([ntfy Docs](https://docs.ntfy.sh/publish/)). An external dead-man check catches the case where the VM dies silently. Keys live in a root-only env file, and paper and live keys stay separate.

"Fast reactions" mostly means fast order management, not fast prediction. The front-run limit order already sits at the broker and fills in microseconds, so what the bot must do quickly is react to the fill. Alpaca's fractional orders are **DAY-only**, and bracket and OCO orders do not combine with fractional quantities ([Alpaca Orders](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/orders-at-alpaca); [Alpaca Forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/frustration-with-fractional-notional-order-limitations-oco-bracket-orders/16277)). On every fill event the bot therefore submits its own stop immediately. It cancels the stop before posting any take-profit sell, so it never double-sells. It re-posts the resting entries and protective stops each morning. Overnight gaps stay unprotected regardless. Whole-share positions in PLTR and CCJ can use native GTC brackets that survive bot downtime, which is worth the rounding. Deterministic `client_order_id`s (symbol-setup-date-leg) and look-up-before-resend logic prevent duplicates after timeouts. The fast loop must be deterministic Python. Claude runs only on triggers such as new filings, pre-entry news checks and post-mortems. A $5–10 monthly API bill is a **6–12% annual drag** on $1,000, which the trading has to earn back first.

## No pause after bad days means the sizing rule carries all the risk

The owner's choice to keep trading after a bad day has more support than the earlier advice implied. In Man Group's 10,000 simulations of a strategy with a genuine edge, a rule of stopping at a 20% drawdown and re-entering at breakeven **underperformed staying invested by 2.2% a year** ([Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/is-this-time-different)). That analysis assumes independent returns, and the authors manage trend-following money, so they have a stake in the answer. A day or week loss brake does not raise returns. It exists to stop tilt and runaway code. Coval and Shumway found that traders with morning losses took more risk in the afternoon ([CFA Digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2005/11/do-behavioral-biases-affect-prices-digest-summary)). The answer that respects the owner's preference is to remove the *P&L-based* pause and handle those two dangers directly. Size every trade as a fixed fraction of *current* equity, so dollar risk shrinks automatically after losses and grows after gains while the bot keeps trading. Make that fraction config that neither Claude nor the bot can raise. Add **operational kill switches** that trip on anything abnormal rather than on losing: repeated order rejections, stale data, a position larger than config allows, more orders in an hour than any setup can generate, or a fill without a matching stop within seconds. Those stops catch the real disasters, the bug that buys ten times or the agent that doubles down, without ever benching a working setup after an ordinary losing day. The cost of this choice should be stated plainly. With no brake, a correlated gap across all three stocks lands in full. At half-account sizing, the simulation's median drawdown is already 17–19%.

The core and trading split is where the account's dependable return comes from. The overnight test showed that holding is the only way to collect these stocks' overnight drift. The session's core test then checked a trend overlay: a weekly 200-day rule with a 3% buffer band. Cutting the core to **half weight below the trend cost 2.5–5.5% a year against always holding but trimmed maximum drawdown by 6–11 points**. Going fully to cash was **worse on both** ([own backtest, Yahoo data](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/PLTR/history/)). An earlier daily all-in/all-out version had looked better for MU and PLTR. That disagreement shows how sensitive trend rules are to settings, and the newer test should take priority. The overlay is insurance with a price, not free return. Since the owner prioritizes growth, holding the core untouched is defensible. The half-weight rule is the option if drawdowns become intolerable. On sizing between sleeves, the owner's goal argues for a larger trading sleeve than the 60–80% core the base rates suggest. The half-account hourly positions need about $500 free per trade, so a **50/50 split** is the natural starting point. Move toward a larger trading sleeve only after the live record beats holding the same three names net of everything. The core is concentrated in its own right: an equal-weight basket of the three ran about **48% annualized volatility**, and each stock has suffered drawdowns of **−85% to −91%** ([Yahoo data](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)).

| Rule | Setting |
|---|---|
| Sleeves | Start ~50% core (held; optional half-weight below a weekly 200-day rule with 3% buffer) / ~50% trading; grow trading only on net outperformance vs holding |
| Live setups | Hourly-level front-run (reward ≥ 1.5x, hold ≤ 3 days, pre-committed) plus daily-level; everything else in shadow mode |
| Size | Hourly: up to ~50% of current equity per position (~0.85% risk with a 1.6% stop); daily-level: ≤ 0.5–1% risk; ≤ 2 concurrent positions; ≤ 35% per name |
| Brakes | No day/week P&L pause; fixed-fraction-of-current-equity sizing; operational kill switches only; risk fraction editable only by owner |
| Events | Flat or reduced into earnings; respect early closes; no entries after fresh negative news |
| Costs | Limit orders; log fill vs midpoint; backtests at 0.10% and 0.20% round trip, since real costs ran **0.07–0.46%** across brokers ([Schwarz et al., JF 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v80y2025i5p2507-2541.html)) |
| Cash rules | Track settled cash; never sell a position bought with unsettled proceeds before settlement; margin off until $2,000+ |
| Tax | Swing profits are short-term, taxed as ordinary income; long-term rate 0% up to $48,350 taxable income for single filers (2025) and losses offset only $3,000 of income a year ([IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)); flag wash sales on every re-entry within 30 days ([Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/taxes/604947/stocks-and-wash-sale-rule)) |

Costs decide whether the hourly setup survives. At a 0.20% round trip instead of 0.10%, its edge falls from about +0.27% to about +0.17% per trade before any decay. Wash sales will fire constantly when the bot re-buys the same three tickers within days. A year that looks flat can still owe tax in December, so the per-lot log must carry the wash-sale adjustment.

## Conclusion

The search for more trades changed the picture in a useful way. Frequency turns out to matter less as a source of profit than as a source of *information*. The hourly-level setup's main gift is that it generates enough fills to measure adverse selection, slippage and per-stock behavior within weeks, even though confirming its edge statistically takes about two years. The session's tests also redraw the map of where returns live: overnight, in held positions. Every rule that tried to harvest the day session lost, and the one that worked depends on holding overnight. That points to a bot that is a patient short-swing trader wrapped around a held core, not a faster day trader.

For the +5%-a-week goal, the evidence supports a different path than raising today's size. With half-account sizing, a working hourly edge should deliver +5% weeks roughly one week in seven, and around 1% a week on average if the edge holds. Turning that into steady +5% weeks needs three things the bot doesn't have yet: a larger account that clears the $2,000 margin threshold, a live record that survives costs and decay, and additional setups promoted from shadow mode only on evidence. Building the measurement, the post-mortem classifier and the always-on infrastructure now is what makes each of those reachable. Removing the loss brakes is workable as long as the sizing fraction and the kill switches stay outside every agent's reach.
