# Shrink the target, harden the bot

The evidence does not support +5% a week. That rate compounds to about **12.6x a year (+1,164%)**, roughly 30 times the **39.1% net annual return** of Renaissance's Medallion Fund, the best track record on record ([WealthManagement.com](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/alternative-investments/jim-simons-built-the-best-hedge-fund-ever)). Every documented attempt at that pace, by human day traders or live LLM agents, ends mostly in losses. The bot can still be improved a lot, but the improvements are about survival and measurement, not speed. Keep most of the $1,000 in a long-term core. Run a small active sleeve that risks 0.5–1% of equity per trade. Use support levels to decide *where to place* orders, not *whether* to trade, and build in protection against resting limit buys getting filled just before price falls through. Treat options and "whale" data as volatility and risk filters, because free public data cannot actually see whales. Use Claude to judge whether news is new, not to race institutions to headlines. On Alpaca, the bot has to manage its own exits, because fractional orders are DAY-only and cannot be bracketed. One cost is easy to miss: a $5–10 monthly API bill is a **6–12% annual drag** on $1,000, so the active sleeve starts behind. The one honest scoreboard is whether the bot beats simply holding the same three stocks, after costs and taxes, with smaller drawdowns.

## Five percent a week means 12.6x a year, and nothing on record does that

Compounding makes the target concrete. At 1.05^52, $1,000 becomes about $12,600 in one year, about $160,000 in two and about $2 million in three. A 1% weekly gain is +68% a year, and 0.5% a week is +30%. Both would already be exceptional ([portfolio notes, own calc; Medallion comparison via WealthManagement.com](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/alternative-investments/jim-simons-built-the-best-hedge-fund-ever)). Medallion averaged **66.1% gross and 39.1% net** from 1988 to 2018 with the best quantitative team ever assembled.

The base rates for retail active trading are worse. Barber and Odean followed 66,465 discount-broker households. The heaviest traders earned **11.4% a year while the market returned 17.9%**, and the authors attribute the overtrading to overconfidence ([Journal of Finance 2000](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:55:y:2000:i:2:p:773-806)). In Taiwan, **fewer than 1% of day traders** reliably earned positive abnormal returns after fees ([Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v18y2014icp1-24.html)). Among Brazilians who day traded for at least 300 days, **97% lost money**, and the authors found "no evidence that individuals learn from day trading" ([Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fgv/eesptd/525.html)). That last result undercuts the hope that the bot or its owner will improve fast enough to justify early losses.

These stocks do produce big weeks, which makes the target feel reachable. In Yahoo daily data from 2005 onward, CCJ, MU and PLTR rose 5% or more in **19%, 24% and 30% of weeks**, but fell 5% or more in **16%, 18% and 25%** of weeks ([Yahoo Finance data, researcher's calculation](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)). Their betas against SPY run about 1.9 to 3.4. A few +5% weeks in a rising market mostly reflect that leverage to the market, not skill.

Prudent sizing also makes the target impossible. Recent ATR(14) runs about 6–9% of price for these names ([Yahoo data, researcher's calculation](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)). Risking 1% ($10) with a 2-ATR stop allows positions of only about **$50–110**, and positions that small cannot move a $1,000 account 5% in a week. The only way to "hit" the target is to size up until drawdowns become ruinous. In the researchers' simulation of a modest positive-edge system, raising risk from 1% to 5% per trade raised the chance of a 50% drawdown from about 0% to about 57%. That result assumes the edge is real, which is unproven.

Costs then widen the gap. A controlled study placing about 85,000 simultaneous orders across five brokers found **round-trip execution costs of 0.07% to 0.46%** even with zero commissions ([Schwarz et al., Journal of Finance 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v80y2025i5p2507-2541.html)). On a 1–2% scalp target, that is 4–46% of gross profit. Add the API bill, which costs the account 6–12% a year before any trade, against SPY's roughly 11% long-run CAGR. The active sleeve must clear all of that just to break even with doing nothing. The right reframing is to keep +5% as a stretch-week marker and never use it as a sizing input. The real goal is to beat an equal-weight CCJ/MU/PLTR buy-and-hold net of everything.

## Support levels tell the bot where to put orders, not whether to trade

The strongest evidence for "levels" comes from currency order books, not stocks, but the mechanism maps cleanly onto the front-run limit strategy. Carol Osler analyzed 9,667 conditional orders at a large dealing bank. She found take-profit orders cluster *at* round numbers: **9.3% executed exactly at "00" versus 4.4% of stop-losses**. That makes round numbers "partially reflecting barriers." Stop-loss sell orders cluster *just below* round numbers ([FRBNY Staff Report 125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.html); published in the [Journal of Finance 2003](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:58:y:2003:i:5:p:1791-1819)). Her follow-up showed those stops trigger "in waves," creating self-reinforcing "price cascades" once a level breaks. The effect is small, though: dollar-mark moved **0.061% in the 15 minutes after crossing a round number versus 0.054%** after arbitrary numbers ([FRBNY Staff Report 150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.html)).

That points to a specific design, and it is mostly an order-placement rule rather than a signal. The resting buy goes a few cents or a fraction of ATR *above* the support zone, so it fills ahead of the clustered orders at the level. The stop goes *beyond* the obvious stop cluster, below the round number plus an ATR buffer, not just under the level where cascades start. Applying this to equities is an extrapolation, since Osler's data are FX only. No peer-reviewed equity study of swing-low support was retrieved.

The front-run limit order has a structural flaw the owner should understand. Resting limit buys are **adversely selected**: they tend to fill when price keeps falling through the level and miss when price bounces early. Using records of every individual investor in Finland, Linnainmaa showed that retail investors' apparently poor post-trade returns, including earnings-announcement losses and contrarian trading, "arise mechanically because limit orders are price-contingent and suffer from adverse selection" ([Journal of Finance 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v65y2010i4p1473-1506.html)). The fixes are concrete. Arm the order only after confirmation, such as a close back above the level or a higher low. Alternatively, use a stop-limit buy above a reclaim level, giving up a little price to avoid fills that keep falling. Cancel the resting order if price closes below the zone before it fills. Log every "touched but unfilled" and "filled then failed" event to measure adverse selection directly.

Paper results will flatter this strategy. Alpaca's paper engine does **not simulate queue position for non-marketable limit orders**, latency slippage or market impact ([Alpaca Paper Trading](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading)). A touch of the limit price can count as a fill that would not have happened live. Backtests and paper logs should count a fill only when price trades *through* the limit by at least a tick.

The equity literature also cuts against blind dip-buying. George and Hwang's 52-week-high work, cited in the authors' later paper, finds that **nearness to the 52-week high** predicts returns better than traditional momentum ([George & Hwang, citing their 2004 JF paper](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/tgeorge/papers/gh5-paper.pdf)). Stocks near highs outperform. Buying support in a stock far below its high is the opposite bet. By the researchers' Yahoo-data calculation, which should be verified against a second source, CCJ sat about 52% below its peak on November 17 and MU about 41% ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)).

If the support strategy ends up with a low win rate and large targets, the arithmetic is harsh. Break-even payoff is (1−p)/p: a 20% win rate needs average wins of **4.0R**, and 15% needs **5.67R**. The chance of 20 straight losses is 1.2% at a 20% win rate and 3.9% at 15%. In the researchers' 100-trade simulation, even a genuinely positive 15%-win, 6R system ended at a loss about 46% of the time. A hundred trades cannot separate skill from luck at that win rate. Risk 1% ($10), not 2%, while the sample builds. At 2% the simulated median peak-to-trough drawdown roughly doubles, to about 29–40%.

## Free "whale" data mostly cannot see whales

The most important point about order-flow signals is what is invisible. No public feed shows other traders' resting stops, hidden or iceberg size, dark-pool intentions, who is behind a print, or whether a short sale opens or closes a position. Claims of "stop hunts" or "dark pool accumulation" built on public data are inferences, not observations. What *is* free is a set of noisy, delayed proxies. Where they work, the academic edge sits in small, illiquid or hard-to-borrow stocks, which is the opposite of CCJ, MU and PLTR. The table grades each signal. A = peer-reviewed and robust, B = peer-reviewed but narrow or decaying, C = practitioner only, D = folklore or none found.

Three caveats shape how to use the options rows. First, much of the published option predictability is **stock borrow-fee information**. Excluding high-fee stocks cuts IV-spread and skew predictability by **at least two-thirds** ([Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/n73cyx89gs)). The rest is largely temporary price pressure that, for liquid stocks, shows "nothing significant after day 1" ([DOTS paper](https://www.abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2016/annual-conference/investment-finance/127-informed-trading-in-options-or-price-pressure-in-stocks-connecting-the-dots-in-option-based-return-predictability.pdf)). Skew survives best because its horizon is months and end-of-day snapshots are enough.

Second, the popular GEX formula assumes dealers are long calls and short puts. In retail-call-heavy PLTR, customers are often net call *buyers*, which would flip the sign. The documented effect concerns volatility and autocorrelation, not direction, so GEX belongs in stop-width and size logic, not in buy/sell triggers.

Third, the data plumbing is weak. yfinance chains carry OI, volume, IV and quotes but **no greeks and no history** ([Macroption](https://www.macroption.com/yahoo-finance-options-python/)). OI is published once a day around **06:30 ET** and reflects the prior session ([ThetaData](https://thetadata.net/docs/operations/option_history_open_interest.html)). It also shows neither who bought nor who sold ([OptionCharts](https://optioncharts.io/docs/open-interest)). So every options feature needs months of self-collected snapshots before it can be backtested. The most valuable step here is to start logging chains twice a day now, compute Black-Scholes gamma yourself, and z-score everything against each stock's own history. That last step matters because the papers ranked hundreds of stocks against each other, and a three-stock bot cannot. Insider buys are the cleanest free "smart money" signal. For PLTR, though, Form 4 traffic is dominated by 10b5-1 sales; one fetched November filing had seven planned sale lines and `aff10b5One`=1 ([SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1321655/000182395226000026/wk-form4_1794963692.xml)). Expect only a handful of real signals a year.

## Claude should judge whether news is new, not race to the headline

For large caps, public news is priced in almost immediately. Prices reacted within seconds of CNBC mentions, positive reports were fully reflected within about a minute, and only traders acting within about 15 seconds profited ([Busse & Green via Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/real-time-market-efficiency/)). Post-earnings drift in large stocks has been **absent since 2006** ([Martineau, Critical Finance Review 2022](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0122)). What survives at swing horizons is asymmetric. **Bad news drifts** while extreme moves with *no* news reverse ([Chan 2003](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:70:y:2003:i:2:p:223-260)). Returns on **stale, recycled news reverse the following week**, more so where individual investors trade heavily ([Tetlock 2011](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v24y2011i5p1481-1512.html)). That gives the news agent a defensible job. It scores each story's novelty against the last N stories on that ticker. It slows dip-buying after genuinely negative new information. It flags sharp moves on stale or no news as fade candidates, especially in retail-heavy PLTR. It vetoes technical setups rather than generating them.

The LLM-trading research supports that narrow role and undermines the broader one. GPT-4 headline scores did predict 1–2 day drift out of sample, but the **annualized Sharpe decayed from 6.54 in late 2021 to 1.22 by early 2024**. The strategy was **unprofitable at 20 bp round-trip costs**, and the edge was concentrated in negative news and small stocks ([Lopez-Lira & Tang](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). The multi-agent TradingAgents framework reported Sharpe ratios of 5.6–8.2, but only over a three-month 2024 window with no transaction costs. The authors themselves noted the Sharpe values exceed the expected empirical range ([Xiao et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.20138v4)). A one-year 2025–26 reproduction found TradingAgents' Sharpe fell to **0.22 net**, and it finished at $102.3K against $104.8K for buy-and-hold. The same paper reports FinMem's return dropping about 72% once tests crossed the model's training cutoff ([The Alpha Illusion](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.16895.md)). On a post-cutoff 2025 benchmark, the best agents made 1.9–2.4% versus 0.4% for buy-and-hold, and GPT-5 made 0.3% ([StockBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02209v1)). In live competition, Claude Sonnet 4.5 lost **42%** in Alpha Arena's first season ([Forklog](https://forklog.com/en/four-out-of-six-ai-models-suffer-losses-in-trading-tournament/)). In the US-equities follow-up, seven of eight models lost money ([aiHola](https://aihola.com/article/grok-wins-alpha-arena-trading)).

Two design rules follow. First, any backtest of the agents on dates before the model's training cutoff is contaminated. A lookahead test found the standalone LLM news signal became insignificant once memorization propensity was controlled for ([arXiv 2512.23847](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23847v1)). Validation therefore has to be forward and paper-traded. Second, the LLM's verbal confidence is not a calibrated probability, so it must never size positions directly. The architecture with the best support uses the LLM as an upstream, auditable information extractor, with sizing, risk and execution handled by deterministic code.

The free feed stack is better than most retail traders realize. EDGAR's submissions JSON needs no key, updates with **under one second** of typical delay, and allows 10 requests a second with a declared User-Agent ([SEC EDGAR APIs](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces); [SEC fair access](https://www.sec.gov/os/accessing-edgar-data)). The CIKs are CCJ 1009001, MU 723125 and PLTR 1321655 ([SEC company_tickers.json](https://www.sec.gov/files/company_tickers.json)). MU and PLTR file earnings 8-Ks (Item 2.02) at about **4:02 and 4:06 PM ET** ([data.sec.gov PLTR](https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK0001321655.json); [MU](https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK0000723125.json)). Cameco is a Canadian foreign private issuer. It files **6-K and 40-F rather than 8-K**, so it has no item codes, and its Q3 release hit EDGAR around 6:57 AM ET ([data.sec.gov CCJ](https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK0001009001.json)). Since a retail Claude pipeline will not win the first 15 minutes, the practical earnings rule is to stand aside into the print and reassess the next morning.

Each stock currently has a dominant catalyst the bot should encode. For **CCJ**, the main item is a fresh **US$1 billion at-the-market equity program** filed November 5 ([SEC SUPPL](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1009001/000119312526450176/d817484dsuppl.htm)). That is roughly 3% of market cap at the US$71.68 prospectus price, and agents can sell into rallies. That discount is a good free sentiment gauge. For **MU**, TrendForce forecasts 4Q26 DRAM contract prices **up 10–15% and NAND up 15–20%** ([TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20260930-13258.html)), and management says it has no "line of sight" to supply balance through 2028 ([third-party transcript](https://www.webull.com/news/15672802111996928)). Liquidity is thin and stops slip in those sessions.

## Alpaca's fractional rules force the bot to manage its own exits

Alpaca is the better-documented choice for code-driven execution, but its fractional rules shape the whole design. Fractional orders work for market, limit, stop and stop-limit types **only with DAY time-in-force**; GTC, IOC and FOK are not supported ([Alpaca Orders](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/orders-at-alpaca)). Alpaca's own pages contradict each other here. The fractional page and the API overview still say fractional trades must be market orders ([Fractional](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/fractional-trading); [Trading API](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/trading-api)). The more detailed Orders page lists DAY-only limit, stop and stop-limit, so test in paper before relying on either. Bracket, OCO and OTO orders do not combine with fractional quantities. A user forum thread still reported that "roadblock" in August 2026, with no staff reply ([Alpaca Forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/frustration-with-fractional-notional-order-limitations-oco-bracket-orders/16277)). Notional orders cannot be replaced at all, only canceled and resubmitted. Sequences that would net short a fractional position are rejected outside market hours ([Alpaca Orders](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/orders-at-alpaca)).

The resulting architecture has five parts. Each morning, while the setup is still valid, the bot re-posts a fractional DAY limit (or confirmation stop-limit) buy. When the `TradingStream` trade-updates feed reports a fill ([alpaca-py](https://alpaca.markets/sdks/python/trading.html)), it immediately submits a DAY stop sell for the filled quantity. It handles the take-profit by price monitoring, or by canceling the stop *before* posting a limit sell, so it never double-sells. It re-posts protective stops every session, including pre-market, where only limit orders are accepted. It accepts that overnight gaps are unprotected between the close and the next re-post. The alternative is whole shares. One share of PLTR or CCJ fits inside $1,000, and whole-share orders can use native GTC brackets with server-side exits that survive bot downtime. MU's price may make a whole share impractical. The researchers' data put it near $712, which should be verified. For reliability, the bot should generate a deterministic `client_order_id` such as symbol-setup-date-leg. On a timeout, it should look the order up rather than resend. Alpaca's pages do not document duplicate-ID behavior, so this is standard practice, not a vendor guarantee.

The regulatory backdrop changed in the bot's favor. Alpaca **lifted the Pattern Day Trader rule on June 4, 2026**. It removed day-trade counting and the $25,000 minimum, and the old API fields such as `daytrade_count` were scheduled for removal by July 6 ([Alpaca blog](https://alpaca.markets/blog/finra-retires-the-pdt-rule-introducing-alpacas-new-intraday-margin-framework/)). Alpaca's fractional page still says fractional day trades count toward a day-trade limit, which is stale. FINRA replaced PDT with an intraday margin standard for all margin accounts. Brokers can phase it in through **October 20, 2027** ([WilmerHale](https://wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20260423-sec-approves-amendments-to-finra-rule-4210-replacing-day-trading-margin-requirements-with-a-modernized-intraday-margin-standard)). Margin loans still require **$2,000 minimum equity** ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/06/26/wall-street-scrapped-a-25-year-old-rule-does-it-ma/)). A $1,000 account therefore cannot carry margin at Alpaca, or on Robinhood Gold. Whether Robinhood still applies legacy day-trade limits during its phase-in was not confirmed. In a cash account, T+1 settlement and good-faith violations still apply. Three GFVs in 12 months bring a 90-day settled-cash restriction ([E*TRADE](https://us.etrade.com/knowledge/library/stocks/understanding-cash-account-violations)), so the bot must track settled cash.

The free data tier is enough for this design if signals run on daily bars. Basic gives real-time quotes from **IEX only**, 30 websocket symbols and 200 historical calls a minute. Algo Trader Plus, at $99 a month, adds all exchanges and OPRA options ([Alpaca Market Data](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/about-market-data-api)). That price would be a 119% annual drag on $1,000. The Trading API allows **200 requests a minute per account** and returns 429 above that ([Alpaca Support](https://alpaca.markets/support/usage-limit-api-calls)). To mimic the real account in paper, create a paper account and cap the capital the bot uses at $1,000, since paper starts at $100K ([Alpaca Paper Trading](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading)). Robinhood's agentic trading, launched May 27, 2026, runs external agents through MCP in a separately funded account ([Robinhood Newsroom](https://robinhood.com/us/en/newsroom/robinhood-is-now-open-to-agents/)). It lists take-profit/stop-loss orders, but no researched fill stream, and warns that "certain trades may still require your approval" ([Robinhood Support](https://robinhood.com/us/en/support/articles/robinhood-agents-overview/)). That makes it weaker for unattended stop management.

## A large core and a capped active sleeve, governed by hard rules

No study gives an optimal core/satellite split. The base rates above justify starting with **60–80% in the core** and treating the active sleeve as a capped experiment. The sleeve earns more capital only after it beats holding the same names, net of execution, API costs and taxes, over 50+ closed trades or six-plus months. The owner should know the core is itself concentrated. Pairwise daily correlations run only 0.17–0.46, but an equal-weight basket of the three has about **48% annualized volatility, roughly 3.4x SPY's**. Worst historical drawdowns are −88% for CCJ, −91% for MU and −85% for PLTR ([Yahoo data, researcher's calculation](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)). A broad index core would be the conventional choice. That would change the stated plan, so it is the owner's decision, but it is the single biggest risk reduction available.

A trend filter on the core helps two of the three stocks and hurts the third. In the researchers' in-sample 200-day moving-average backtest (10 bp per switch, cash at 0%), the filter roughly halved maximum drawdown for MU and PLTR without lowering CAGR. On CCJ it was costly: about nine whipsaw switches a year cut CAGR from 5.5% to 0.6%, and it still suffered a −79% drawdown ([Yahoo data, researcher's calculation](https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/MU/history/)).

| Ticker | Buy & hold CAGR / max DD | 200-day filter CAGR / max DD |
|---|---|---|
| SPY | 11.0% / −55% | 7.9% / −22% |
| CCJ | 5.5% / −88% | 0.6% / −79% |
| MU | 21.1% / −91% | 21.3% / −57% |
| PLTR | 45.4% / −79% | 50.5% / −47% |

This matches the published finding that trend rules mainly cut drawdowns rather than add return. Time-series momentum is robust across 58 diversified futures markets ([Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:104:y:2012:i:2:p:228-250)), but noisy in single volatile stocks. The better rule is to *scale* the core rather than switch it on and off: 100% of target above the 200-day average and 50% below, checked monthly with a ±2–3% buffer band. That rule was not backtested. It also limits the tax damage of selling core lots.

Taxes argue strongly for keeping the core truly long-term. Net short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income. Long-term gains qualify for a **0% rate up to $48,350** of taxable income for single filers (2025 thresholds) ([IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)), which a college student may well fall under. Repeatedly trading the same three tickers will trigger **wash sales** constantly. Buying back within 30 days before or after a loss sale defers the loss into the new shares' basis ([Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/taxes/604947/stocks-and-wash-sale-rule)). The bot can report a "flat" year that still owes tax in December. Net capital losses offset only $3,000 of ordinary income a year ([IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)). Kiddie-tax rules for supported full-time students under 24 were not researched and are worth a question to a tax professional.

The rulebook below collects the parameters the evidence supports. Most are heuristics anchored to the risk math rather than proven optima.

| Rule | Setting |
|---|---|
| Allocation | 60–80% core (long-term, scaled by 200-day trend, monthly check); 20–40% active, grown only on net outperformance vs holding the same names |
| Risk per active trade | 0.5–1% of total equity ($5–10); shares = risk ÷ (entry − stop); stop 1.5–2× ATR beyond the stop cluster |
| Exposure caps | ≤25–35% of equity per name; one combined, beta-aware risk budget across all three |
| Kelly | Never computed from the bot's own short history; at most 0.25–0.5 Kelly on shrunk estimates (half-Kelly keeps ~75% of maximum growth) |
| Loss brakes | No new trades after a −2% day; active sleeve pauses after a −4–5% week; halve risk at −10% from peak; stop and review at −20%; no martingale; no cap on winning weeks |
| Event rules | Flat or reduced size into earnings (expected move understates realized uncertainty, per [Gao, Xing & Zhang](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/anticipating-uncertainty-straddles-around-earnings-announcements/7B34877AD5E06304BA3C55FBA3219FDD)); smaller size around FOMC, CPI and early closes |
| Costs | Limit orders only; no market orders at the open or close; log fill vs midpoint; total API spend well under 1% of equity a month, with cheap models for monitoring and the expensive model only on triggers |
| Operations | Pause on API failures, order rejections or data gaps; deterministic order IDs; settled-cash tracking; per-lot log of basis, wash-sale flag, sleeve and rationale |

The asymmetric brake, stopping after losses but never after gains, is the one to encode. No academic study shows weekly loss limits raise returns. Their justification is that overconfidence and the absence of learning, not missing signals, are what destroy retail accounts ([Barber & Odean](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:55:y:2000:i:2:p:773-806); [Chague et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fgv/eesptd/525.html)). Alpha Arena's leveraged, overtrading agents fit that pattern ([Forklog](https://forklog.com/en/four-out-of-six-ai-models-suffer-losses-in-trading-tournament/)).

## Conclusion

The bot's real edge is not a secret signal. Every "whale" and news feature available for free is either a small, decaying effect in exactly the kind of liquid stocks it trades, or folklore. What the bot can do better than a human is enforce discipline without fatigue: placing stops beyond the clusters, refusing to chase retail-attention spikes and stale headlines, standing aside into earnings, tracking wash sales and settled cash, and cutting risk after losing weeks. That reframes the Claude agents' job from forecasting to auditing. They read filings faster than a student can, score novelty, and veto bad setups, while deterministic code owns every number that touches money.

The most valuable thing to build this month is the measurement layer, not another signal. That means twice-daily option-chain snapshots, a log of filled versus missed orders, fill-versus-midpoint slippage, API cost per trade, and a running comparison against holding CCJ, MU and PLTR untouched. Without months of that self-collected history, none of the options or flow features can be tested honestly. With it, the owner will know within two quarters whether the active sleeve deserves more of the $1,000. The likeliest result is that most of the money belongs in the core.
